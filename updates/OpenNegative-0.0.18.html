One thing moves the rendering of photographs you have already adjusted, and it is named first. Sharpening and chroma denoise are now dosed in pixels of the full-resolution scan instead of a fraction of its width. On a scan whose long side is 8368 px — what the old fraction was calibrated on — nothing moves at all. On any other size it does: measured on scans of 6000 to 6240 px, the dose lands 34 to 39 % higher than before. Everything else comes back identical.

Two consequences worth knowing before you install. Every thumbnail is rebuilt once, on the first import or launch, behind a progress step — after that they are never rebuilt again. And a photograph adjusted in this version opens read-only in 0.0.17 and below: going back loses nothing, but the older version will not let you move a handle. Every published version stays in the feed, so going back remains possible.

The gallery no longer freezes

Dragging the scroll bar froze the app for two to five seconds, and macOS reported it as not responding. The cause was the container itself: it filled contiguously, so reaching frame 600 built all 600 on the way, and it paid that cost again on every switch between the grid and the editor.

The grid is now computed rather than measured — every tile's rectangle and the document's height are arithmetic, and only the cells the visible band touches are drawn. Landing on the last frame of a 600-photograph day builds 78 cells instead of 600. The scroll bar can be dragged end to end, and the scroll bar itself is honest, because the height is computed rather than estimated.

Thumbnails are written once

They were regenerated when the app opened, when the gallery reopened, and when the window was resized. They are now written once, at a fixed size, kept in the app's own files, and rebuilt only after you edit the photograph. Deleting a photograph deletes its thumbnails with it.

What you see: a step with a progress bar the first time, then a gallery that opens immediately and stays that way.

The gallery groups by capture date

It grouped by import date, so re-importing or re-scanning part of a roll split it across two headings. It now reads the capture date from the file. A roll stays together whatever evening you scanned it.

Six columns, or twelve

A strip of film is cut into six frames, so a row is a strip; twelve is that strip doubled, for sorting a roll rather than judging one frame. ⌘− and ⌘+ walk between the two, and the change is crossed with a fade instead of photographs sliding to new places.

Both are fully responsive: the count is what you choose, and the tile is what takes the room. On a wide screen the tiles are large. There is no width at which the grid rearranges itself behind your back.

The conversion works in density

Inverting a negative by 1 − x on linear data crushes everything into the first few percent of the range, which is why the handles felt unusable at one end and violent at the other. The whole chain now works in density, where a stop is a stop.

What changes for you: the three per-channel level windows read a density with three decimals instead of a percentage, and their track runs from −0.35 to 3. A black point can be placed under zero, which is where a channel transmitting past 1 actually lands — previously unreachable. Your existing adjustments were converted exactly; the arithmetic is an identity, and it is verified pixel for pixel against the frozen previous version.

A new control: Density ceiling

Scans differ in what they left under zero. The top of the density axis was a fixed constant that suited eight measured films differently, so it became a control — resting exactly on the old constant, so nothing moves until you touch it.

Raising it buys separation in a burnt patch, at the cost of pulling up whatever the scanner left under zero. It is the one handle that reaches a channel piled flat against the floor, which no gain and no black point can separate.

Colour is now Temperature and Tint

Colour was a single gesture that was hard to aim. It is now two named sliders, in the order and the sense every other application uses: pushing right warms. They are shouldered, so the travel is fine near the middle and never runs into a wall at the end.

They stay a finishing gesture and are deliberately independent of the balance gains above them: a finishing move must not displace the conversion, nor the answer an automatic button just gave. Colour density now sits with them, under the same heading, since it belongs to the same decision.

Lighting gains Shadows and Highlights

Exposure and White moved the two ends, and one gamma moved everything between them. That single exponent cannot fold two problems at once: after the medians are aligned, crossed dye curves leave the quarter-tones and the three-quarter-tones apart.

Two sliders now steer those two zones directly, and the same pair was added to the per-channel level windows. The ends stay pinned, so a black and a white placed on data keep their meaning whatever the inner three carry. Everything rests where it did, so nothing moves until you move it.

Three more automatic balance buttons

None of them is right on every photograph, and that is the point — each wins films the others lose, in a measured way. Whites places the white point on a higher quantile than Highs. Body moves both ends together so the tenth and ninetieth quantiles land on green's. AVG is the average position of all five handles across the other five buttons.

AVG is first on nothing and last on nothing, which is what it is for: it is the one to press when you do not want to try five. The buttons that assume nothing about the scene's colour are tinted apart from the ones that align a statistic across the channels.

Sharpening and chroma denoise are dosed in pixels

Both radii were a fraction of the image's width, calibrated on one scanner's output. The same setting therefore meant a different real dose on a scan of another size, silently.

They are now full-resolution pixels: a slider reading 3 px puts 3 px in the export. This is what moves the rendering, and only on scans whose long side is not 8368 px. Their ranges were tightened to what a preview can actually show — the bottom of the sharpening track is now the smallest radius a fitted preview can display at all, so no part of the gesture is inert on screen while still reaching the export. Use the loupe to judge the real dose: a fitted preview cannot show it, whatever the unit.

Navigating the photograph

Dragging now follows the pointer instead of moving against it. The wheel zooms in sixteen steps across the range rather than the barely perceptible amount it moved before. A double click goes to 100 %, and 100 % now really shows the pixels at any window size — the readout used to say "100 % · preview" on a large display while showing a reduced copy.

Explanations moved into the panels

Notes under blocks are gone. A question mark now sits to the right of each section title and opens a bubble on hover, drawn above the panel and confined to the picture area, so it never covers the control it describes. Every sentence was rewritten to say what the control does for you rather than how it works.

Smaller things

A resized export no longer leaves a translucent rim around its edge.

The window can no longer be dragged narrower than its own contents, which used to cut the interface off on the right instead of refusing.

Lighting's Shadows and Highlights sliders lighten to the right, like Exposure and White beside them. Only the handle was reflected: the stored value, the sidecar and the rendering do not move.

What it runs on

macOS 13 Ventura or newer, on Apple Silicon or Intel — one universal build for both. The interface is dressed for recent macOS; on older systems it is the same app with a plainer toolbar.
