Your photographs render exactly as before. No stage changed shape and no setting changed value: a conversion already made comes back identical, pixel for pixel. This version adds a third mode next to Negative and Positive.

A black-and-white mode

B&W joins Negative and Positive in the top bar, because it is the same kind of thing: what the frame is, not a treatment you dial. It inverts and rests exactly like a negative, because that is what it is — printed grey.

It adds no channel mixer, and that is deliberate. The three sliders in the Colour block already do that work: each places one point at the middle of its channel's curve, never touching the black or the white point. Push red and the sky darkens — that is the Tri-X yellow filter, reached with a control you already know. The projection to grey happens only at the very end, and does nothing but render what those curves prepared.

Put differently: all the colour work still happens. It is what decides which grey.

What the mode takes away

Anything that only shapes colour leaves the screen rather than sitting there doing nothing:

  • The Grade tab — the finishing balance and zone saturation act on a chroma the last stage projects away regardless.
  • The Mids and Highs buttons of the automatic balance: aligning medians or highlights across channels shapes a cast. What remains is Equalise, which places each channel's ends and gives a clean base.
  • The Color density slider. Its value is kept and returns if you switch back to colour.

Cropping, the automatic balance, the three colours, the Lighting block, flat-field correction and the whole Detail tab all stay. The manual conversion is still there behind its door, called manual equalisation here.

Also

  • A photo set to black and white and then opened by an earlier version opens as a negative instead of refusing to open at all. Its settings are intact; only the mode is ignored by a version that does not know it.
